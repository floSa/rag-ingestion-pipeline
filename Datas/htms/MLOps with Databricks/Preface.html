Preface

I have been working in Data and AI for over a decade, and I’ve devoted most of my career to machine learning operations (MLOps). I started doing it before MLOps became a formalized term in the late 2010s. Even though MLOps is an established term today, too few companies and teams follow proper MLOps principles.

In many teams I encounter today, there tends to be an imbalance in expertise. Some team members have deep experience in data science and know how to build and monitor machine learning (ML) models, but they are less familiar with development and operations (DevOps) practices. Others come from a strong software engineering background and follow DevOps principles rigorously, yet they often treat the ML or AI component as a black box.

Neither situation is inherently problematic, but both present challenges when the team’s goal is to build reliable ML/AI systems that enable other teams or the organization to continuously deliver value at an accelerated pace. Success requires bridging the gap: acknowledging the nondeterministic nature of many ML/AI components while also applying the discipline of DevOps practices.

This book focuses on traditional ML. About a third of it is devoted to large language model operations (LLMOps), which is a specialized subset of MLOps that focuses on applications built around large language models and, more broadly, foundational and multimodal generative models.

In that sense, MLOps provides the foundation: the principles and practices for building, deploying, and maintaining ML systems. These principles and practices extend naturally to newer classes of models, even as the emphasis often shifts from training to inference.

While foundational models have unlocked powerful new capabilities and are rapidly being adopted across a wide range of use cases, traditional ML is not going away. Many real-world systems continue to rely on it, and the core principles behind it still underpin modern AI applications.

My goal is to help to professionalize the field of ML (and AI, as a broader concept) and make the AI systems we build more reliable and robust, so science teams can deliver value faster and with higher levels of trust.

Why Databricks?

I believe MLOps is not about tools but about principles. However, I strongly believe the best way to learn MLOps is with hands-on practice, using the tools directly. This is necessary because different interconnected components are required to support these principles. No single tool is perfect, and all of them are tailored to the most common use cases their users deal with.

When companies are designing their MLOps platform, they must choose between two options:

  1. They can build the end-to-end planform from separate heterogeneous best-of-breed tools—and then face all the complications that come with combining all the tools together. Organizations that choose this path often end up going for a combination of open source tools and closed source vendors.

  2. Alternatively, they can use an existing “all-in-one” MLOps platform vendor.

This is a tough choice, and which option is the right one depends on many factors, such as the knowledge level and size of the team maintaining the platform, the specifics of the infrastructure setup (e.g., on premises, multi cloud, single cloud), what other tools the company uses, and who would be the end users of the platform.

Various “all-in-one” MLOps platforms exist. There’s Azure ML, AWS Sagemaker, Vertex AI, and Databricks, among others. Even though none of them is perfect and they all have their own pros and cons, for me, Databricks has stood out from the pack throughout the years.

Databricks was founded by the creators of Apache Spark, and historically, it has been used widely by data engineering teams. Delta Lake was originally developed by Databricks, and it is the most widely used lakehouse storage format in the world. With its acquisition of Tabular, a company behind Apache Iceberg, and the open sourcing of Delta, Databricks has reinforced its commitment to open source. Databricks also created Unity Catalog, which it released as open-source software (OSS) in 2024. It’s a governance layer that provides centralized access control, discoverability, and lineage for all data assets (including models). Since data is at the heart of any ML and AI system, it simplifies collaboration between data engineering and ML/AI teams.

As the ML field has evolved, Databricks has emerged as one of the leading players in MLOps, with many of its tools having become prevalent in the production ML space. In fact, Databricks was ranked in the top three across most categories in the 2025 State of Production ML Survey from the Institute for Ethical AI & Machine Learning.

One flagship tool is MLflow, which is well known and widely used. It was one of the first tools for tracking experiments and registering models. However, not everyone knows that MLflow was developed by Databricks and comes integrated with its enterprise platform’s workspaces as a managed MLflow service, which simplifies a lot of aspects of MLOps.

Finally, Databricks has also acquired Tecton,1 which is one of the leading players in feature store engines. It closes the gap between the data lake and ML data pipelines.

Databricks’ focus on open source and making these tools part of its platform makes Databricks a strong player in the data and AI field. These are some of the reasons why Databricks has been my tool of choice for this book. This preference of mine is also shaped by my extensive hands-on experience using Databricks in real-world settings, both as an engineer and through my consulting work. Over the years, I’ve led the deployment of production ML/AI systems, supported MLOps and LLMOps adoption in large enterprises, and trained hundreds of practitioners through courses and workshops.

So having worked across a range of tools and platforms, my preference for Databricks is not just based on my familiarity with it from using it extensively. I’ve repeatedly seen where it stands out from the crowd when the challenge shifts from building AI demos to operating AI systems reliably in production.

Who and What This Book Is For

This is a technical book. That means you should have some working experience in data science, data, ML, or AI engineering. All the code covered in the book is written in Python, which is commonly used for developing AI applications. It makes it easier to go through the book if you have already worked with Databricks before, but it’s not a prerequisite.

This is also a book with a lot of code. To benefit from it the most, you should dive into the code and try it out step by step. On the other hand, I do not want you to execute the code blindly. That’s why this book focuses on the MLOps principles first and explains how using different components of Databricks in the right way helps support these principles.

This book also covers architectures and offers different considerations for why a certain architecture would be a good choice for a particular use case. Data and AI architects will also benefit from the book even if they don’t try out the code.

Writing a technical book covering different platform features of Databricks is not easy. Based on my experience, this is one of the fastest-evolving products. I realize that parts of the book may become outdated, and I hope to minimize this risk by taking a “principles first” approach. This means that although some of the code, tools, and technical details may become out of date, the theoretical foundations and best practices presented should not. I believe that the book will help a lot of data and AI professionals improve their ML workflows by making them more robust and reliable.

I have written this book as the handbook I wish I’d had when I was first getting started with Databricks. One of the pain points I’ve found is that while there are plenty of examples of how to use different components of Databricks, there are very few examples that bring a cohesive view of how all these components actually work together. I hope this book will provide those examples for you, save you a lot of time, and help you on your MLOps journey.

How This Book Is Organized

The book focuses on two main use cases: traditional ML application (which I use to demonstrate the concepts I present in Chapters 2 through 6) and an LLM-based application (which I cover in Chapters 7 through 9). It also covers how to deploy these applications in a robust way.

In Chapter 1, I start the book by breaking down the MLOps principles and components that are relevant for any AI application.

In Chapter 2, I take you on a deep dive into different options for developing an application on Databricks. I also explain the relevant details of the Databricks infrastructure, and I highlight the importance of following best software engineering practices.

In Chapter 3, I go through the main components of MLflow for traditional ML, and I showcase how you can use these components for experiment tracking and registering the models. MLflow is tightly connected to the Model Serving feature on Databricks.

In Chapter 4, I discuss the different Model Serving architectures and when you should use each of them, and I go through the practical implementation of those architectures.

In Chapter 5, I focus on the deployment of the whole ML pipeline using Declarative Automation Bundles. The chapter brings together all the code that we discussed in the previous chapters and shows how all the steps of the pipeline are related to each other. We will also talk about Databricks platform design, deployment strategies, and testing.

In Chapter 6, I cover the Data Quality Monitoring functionality. I highlight the importance of monitoring and show you how to use inference tables to simplify monitoring for Model Serving endpoints.

Chapters 7 through 9 form a special section in which I focus on the LLM capabilities of Databricks and show you how the principles I cover throughout the book apply to AI applications in practice. I specifically wanted to include this material because of the industry’s shift from traditional ML toward AI systems and the growing demand around them. Earlier drafts of this book covered all of this in just one chapter, but the operational surface turned out to be too large to do justice to it all in one place, so I split it into three. Each chapter shows, in practice, how much of what we covered in Chapters 2 through 6, also applies to LLM-based applications. Chapter 7 covers foundation models and context engineering. Chapter 8 turns to MLflow for GenAI and how it supports evaluation, tracing, and the development workflow for these systems. Chapter 9 covers deploying and monitoring these systems in production. This includes cost monitoring, since for GenAI, workload spend is one of the operational signals you must watch the closest.

In Chapter 10, I explore how to navigate AI regulations, implement security best practices, and establish model governance using the Databricks platform. I also tie these topics back to the operational, deployment, and lifecycle management principles discussed throughout the book.

Shows commands or other text that should be typed literally by the user.

Constant width italic
